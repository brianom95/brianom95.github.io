It had been an adventurous few weeks on [Aconcagua](aconcagua) and the [O-Trek](otrek). Deeper into Patagonia was my next big hike, the notorious Huemul Circuit in El Chaltén, Argentina. This is often called Patagonia's most difficult hike. All I knew beforehand was the terrain is rough and slow, some parts are on a glacier, and big rivers are crossed by tyrolean traverses where you pull yourself across on a wire. That was enough to convince me.

## Introduction

The Huemul Circuit is a remote loop in Argentina’s Los Glaciares National Park, beginning and ending in El Chaltén. Most people hike it in four days, camping at Laguna Toro, Paso del Viento, Bahia de los Témpanos and Lago Viedma, though I combined the first two days and completed it in three. The route crosses rough scree slopes, muddy forests, glacier viewpoints and two tyrolean traverses suspended above glacial rivers. 

What makes the hike famous is its proximity to the Southern Patagonian Ice Field; the third largest outside Antarctica and Greenland. Huge glaciers spill down into valleys filled with turquoise lakes and floating icebergs. Weather changes constantly and strong winds are challenging. Compared to the crowds around Fitz Roy (the most polular hike in Argentina), very few people hike the Huemul Circuit. It still feels wild.

## Arrival in Chalten

I arrived late into El Chaltén and found my hostel. Originally I planned to hike to Fitz Roy first, but a huge storm was forecast to arrive in a few days so I changed my plans. There was a perfect three day weather window starting the next morning, which would allow me to combine the first two stages and finish before the storm hit.

Gear rental in Patagonia is unbelievably expensive, which made me glad I already had my own tent, sleeping bag and mat. I still needed to rent a climbing harness and carabiners for the tyrolean traverses though. Honestly, you could probably buy them cheaper elsewhere than renting them here for a few days.

I stocked up on expensive trail food in town, mostly for cold soaking meals again, and waited until 9:00am for the rental shops to open before finally setting off around 10:00am.

A new national park entry fee had just been introduced for Los Glaciares National Park, costing roughly ARS 45,000 (around $30 USD at the time). Thankfully their card systems were offline when I arrived so everyone waiting at the entrance was waved through for free. Patagonia finally gave me a win.

## Day 1
#### El Chaltén to Paso del Viento
*Distance: 30.2km | Ascent: 1,620m*

Near the trailhead there’s a ranger hut where you fill out a form in case you go missing, then show proof before being allowed continue. The ranger wished me luck and I headed into the valley beneath huge granite peaks similar to Fitz Roy.

The first section was fast and mostly pleasant. Nice lake views opened beside the trail while snowy mountains dominated the horizon. I finished the standard “Day 1” section in roughly three hours and stopped briefly at the campsite beside Laguna Toro where I met some climbers from Colorado descending from another route nearby.

From there the trail became rougher. Steep rocky climbs, loose scree and sections where the route became difficult to follow. Then came the first tyrolean traverse.

It was terrifying.

The river below was violent and unbelievably loud. I’d watched videos the night before explaining how to cross safely, especially how to clip your backpack correctly, which I’d strongly recommend because once you’re hanging above the water it’s easy to doubt yourself. Pulling myself across was exhausting and filled with adrenaline. I was very relieved to reach the far side.

The weather turned windier as I climbed higher onto the glacier section. Deep crevasses split the ice and footing needed care, but the views became more spectacular with every step. The Southern Patagonian Ice Field stretched endlessly into the distance while another glacier filled the next valley beyond the pass.

Bright mineral coloured rocks surrounded the upper valley along with small alpine lakes. I descended quickly on loose scree towards Paso del Viento campsite, tucked between rocks beside the lake for shelter from the wind. There were only six other tents there, everyone trying to beat the incoming storm.

Some people swam in the freezing lake while I filled water, chatted to an Australian guy camped nearby, then hid in my tent once rain arrived. Snacks, downloaded movies and the sound of wind outside the tent. I had missed this feeling while travelling.

## Day 2
#### Paso del Viento to Bahia de los Témpanos
*Distance: 15.1km | Ascent: 530m*

Today looks short on paper but definitely isn’t. The trail traverses high scree slopes overlooking the glacier, constantly climbing and descending over rough terrain. I was the last person to leave camp so I kept catching up to people throughout the morning and chatting briefly before moving on again.

The glacier views became more impressive the higher I climbed, especially approaching the final endless pass above Lago Viedma. On the far side began one of the worst descents I’ve ever done.

Steep mud, slippery roots and loose rock the entire way down. Even in mostly dry conditions it was awful and genuinely had me cursing out loud at points. Calling this the hardest hike in Patagonia is probably an exaggeration, but this descent certainly argues its case.

Eventually grass and beaches replaced the scree. Icebergs floated across the lake while long green peninsulas stretched into the water below. The scenery completely changed again.

I camped directly beside the lakeshore, dried my gear in the sun and sat with my feet in the freezing water. It was one of the nicest camp spots of the whole trip and made all the hardship worthwhile. Only two tents were setup there while everyone else camped higher near the main trail.

That evening strong rain and wind returned so I retreated into the tent for another movie night with snacks while Patagonia battered the fabric outside.

## Day 3
#### Bahia de los Témpanos to El Chaltén
*Distance: 25.2km | Ascent: 920m*

I woke to a horrible surprise. A mouse had chewed through my tent overnight and eaten my wraps. This happened to me years earlier on the West Highland Way and apparently I still hadn’t learned my lesson about storing food beside lakes.

My panic while checking every corner of the tent and my backpack to make sure the mouse was gone would probably have looked ridiculous. Thankfully the damage was mostly limited to my lunch.

The morning was gloomy and extremely windy. A major storm was forecast to arrive that evening and last several days so it definitely felt like the right time to leave. I moved quickly through grassy valleys, muddy bog sections and long beach stretches while fog slowly lifted from the mountains.

The second tyrolean traverse had a huge queue where people struggled clipping themselves and their bags onto the cable. They let me pass ahead and afterwards I showed a few people how to secure their backpacks properly so they wouldn’t lose water bottles into the river halfway across.

The final section felt endless. Strong headwinds turned the trail into a treadmill and the last kilometres followed a road back towards town. Eventually El Chaltén appeared ahead along with the promise of a warm shower and real food.

I celebrated with a burger and a pint before returning to the hostel feeling very proud of the last few days. That night the storm arrived properly and hammered the town until morning. Timing wise, it couldn’t have worked out better.

## Afterthoughts

The Huemul Circuit is a brilliant hike. Harder than most trails in Patagonia, but probably not as terrifying as people online make it sound. Still, it shouldn’t be underestimated. There’s no signal for most of the route and rescue would be a long way away if something went wrong.

The tyrolean traverses, glacier travel, steep muddy descents and brutal weather all add genuine challenge. Strong winds and freezing temperatures make camp life feel serious too. But that’s exactly what makes the route memorable.

More than anything, I loved how remote it felt. Compared to the crowds around Fitz Roy and Torres Del Paine, the Huemul still feels adventurous. The final campsite beside the lake is one of the prettiest places I’ve ever pitched a tent.
