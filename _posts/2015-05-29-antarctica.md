*The Antarctica Marathon is one of the most remote races on Earth. 100 runners sail south from Ushuaia every year on the infamous Drake Passage, with hopes of favourable weather on what is for many the seventh continent. It's a long way to travel for a race, but the marathon is only one part of the adventure.*

This was the final race in my journey to join the Seven Continents Club.

## Introduction

This race had a three year waiting list, which gave me enough time to race the other continents first. Three years ago it seemed impossibly far away, but now as I backpacked my way through South America it grew undeniably closer. It had been the centre of this trip in many ways, the reason why I had moved south since Mexico. Always on my mind through hikes on Aconcagua and in Patagonia. The biggest goal I'd hoped to achieve before I turn 30 later that year.

The trip cost me $9,000, although I got lucky booking early. Prices had risen to over $10,500 by departure. There are occasionally cheaper last-minute deals available in  if you're willing to wait in Ushuaia and ask every day, but there are no guarantees. The marathon itinerary only includes a few planned days in Antarctica too, so if your goal is simply sightseeing then I'd recommend a longer expedition. For me though, the race was the draw and I couldn't wait to finally reach the seventh continent.

## Buenos Aires

I arrived in Buenos Aires a few days before the group and was glad of it. After staying in America del Sur Hostel I had plenty of time to settle in, explore the city by bike, wander the markets and celebrate St. Patrick's Day in an Irish pub with a great crowd. The food was excellent, the weather was hot and after weeks on the move it felt good to slow down for a few days before the expedition began.

The race package included three nights in a five-star hotel and moving there felt like stepping into a different world. My roommate was Vitaly and our room was far fancier than anything I'd stayed in recently. The first group run was only 5km but it gave us a chance to meet people. I chatted to Nic, a journalist whose work had appeared in National Geographic. He'd spent years mountaineering before becoming a very fast marathon runner. I also met race organisers Ann and Tinny, along with Paul who was one of the race medics and somehow managed to balance being a doctor with being one of the most relaxed people on the trip.

The following day we toured Buenos Aires in a convoy of buses, visiting many of the places I'd already seen. It was during this that I met John from the UK, who quickly became the person I was most impressed by on the ship. He had placed top five in all six World Marathon Majors, run sub-three hours in every one, held a UK marathon record for his age group and completed a 100 miler. He spoke about all of it so casually you'd nearly miss how extraordinary it was. There were plenty of other interesting people too. Anton from Australia held multiple world records, Kevin was great company, and Karen from the UK had retired to Thailand and was now travelling the world. Everywhere you sat there seemed to be another fascinating story.

That evening we completed race registration, attended welcome cocktails and listened to presentations on the trip ahead. As the wine flowed and people got to know each other, I realised this expedition was going to be as much about the people as Antarctica itself.

## The Journey South

An early flight brought us to Ushuaia, the southernmost city in the world. I sat beside Tommy, an American librarian who was exceptionally well spoken and became my roommate for the ship. The cold wind hit us as soon as we landed and it immediately felt different to Buenos Aires. Patagonia was behind us and Antarctica suddenly felt close.

The ship was waiting in port and looked enormous. I'd booked the cheapest cabin available, deep inside the vessel with only a small porthole, but it was more comfortable than I could have hoped for. Tommy and I quickly explored everything with John, finding the gym, jacuzzis, bars, sauna, lecture hall and the mud room where we'd prepare for Antarctic landings. As we departed, Captain Mike welcomed us aboard. He was Scottish and quickly became one of the familiar voices of the trip, joined by his two senior officers who happened to be Russian and Ukrainian. Thousands of kilometres from politics, everyone simply got on with the job.

Unfortunately the Drake Passage had other ideas. Severe weather prevented us leaving the Beagle Channel and what should have been a short delay stretched into several days. At first nobody minded. The food was excellent, there was a packed schedule of lectures and the expedition staff were brilliant. We learned about Antarctic wildlife, photography, exploration history and geology. Biosecurity briefings explained how seriously Antarctica protects itself from contamination, with every pair of boots requiring careful cleaning before landings. One of the most memorable presenters was affectionately known as Dr Volcano, whose geology lectures covered billions of years of history and enough rocks to make me feel like I was back in college.

As the days passed though, concern started to grow. Every missed day ate into our Antarctic schedule and there was genuine uncertainty around whether the marathon would happen at all. We spent evenings at quizzes, karaoke and chatting in the bar. My team name one night was Make Antarctica Great Again. We somehow finished third. Despite the delay, I was enjoying every minute. The people made it easy.

Eventually the weather improved and we sailed south. The Drake Passage lived up to its reputation. Even with seasickness tablets I felt drowsy most of the time and Tommy wasn't much better. We mostly surfaced for lectures and meals before retreating back to the cabin. The best talk came from Will, our historian, who spoke about Antarctic exploration. Every time whales were spotted the intercom would announce it and everyone on board would sprint for the nearest window. On the second day the seas finally calmed and we spotted our first iceberg. That evening we received our race briefing. The delay meant everything would happen quickly. The marathon team would land first thing in the morning to set up the course and we'd follow shortly afterwards. If the weather deteriorated, the race could still be cancelled.

## The Race

I woke early and looked out the porthole. After days staring at open ocean there was finally land outside. Antarctica. The ship rolled quietly into a bay on King George Island while the marathon staff loaded zodiacs with equipment and headed ashore. Soon afterwards we were called to the mud room, pulling on heavy outer layers, lifejackets and wellies before climbing into black zodiacs. The ride felt surreal. The air was icy, the water dark and the whole thing felt more like a military operation than a marathon.

The course was based around the research stations of Chile, Russia and China. By March much of the coastline is thawed so rather than snow we found mud, rock and rough ground. We arrived in running gear beneath our dry layers, using tarps to change and organise nutrition before the countdown began. Conditions weren't too bad at around -2°C, but the wind was strong and would become the defining feature of the day.

The marathon consisted of three laps and was tougher than I'd expected. The terrain was rough, there were sections of wet rock beside the shoreline and headwinds that seemed to come from every direction. The scenery more than made up for it though. The landscape felt harsh and alien, unlike anywhere I'd ever been. Running past the Chinese station my phone connected briefly and welcomed me to China, which felt appropriately ridiculous while racing in Antarctica.

One of the nicest aspects of the race was repeatedly crossing paths with people I'd spent the previous week getting to know. Anton shouted "Sláinte!" every time he saw me, John looked unbelievably strong, and a US Senator greeted me every lap with an increasingly tired "Hi Brian". Nathan slipped in the mud at one point and spent the rest of the marathon covered from head to toe. There were penguins near the start and more near the Chinese station, and I stopped several times just to watch them. It wasn't my fastest marathon, but it was certainly my most unique.

The final lap was hard. The winds seemed to strengthen and I quietly worried the race might be stopped before everyone got through. I was tired, cold and ready to finish, but the final turnaround gave confidence that I would make it. Crossing the line felt incredible. Years of planning, weeks travelling through South America and days of uncertainty at sea had finally paid off. Most runners returned directly to the ship afterwards but I stayed ashore for another hour. Ab, our penguin expert, brought me over to a nearby colony and before long we found ourselves surrounded by curious birds investigating us from every angle. Back on board, the hot shower felt amazing and the atmosphere at dinner that night was even better.

## Antarctica

The following morning we woke at Hannah Point and headed ashore by zodiac. Huge penguin colonies covered the hillsides and the smell hit us long before we reached them. Most were Gentoo penguins with some Chinstraps mixed in. The males were still building nests from rocks and half seemed to spend their day collecting stones while the others simply stole them from neighbouring nests. The result was constant squabbling and surprisingly entertaining to watch. We also saw large seals stretched across beaches and entire groups huddled together while moulting. Antarctica wasn't the frozen wasteland I'd imagined. It felt alive.

The highlight of the trip came later that day. Long before Antarctica I realised I'd kayaked on six continents and this trip offered the chance to complete the seventh. Unfortunately there were only a handful of spaces available and over one hundred people wanted them. I emailed beforehand, spoke to staff beforehand and did everything possible to improve my chances. It all came down to a lottery. Somehow I was drawn in Group 2, then weather delays reduced the number of kayaking opportunities to almost nothing. By sheer luck, the only group that eventually got out was mine.

We launched near Deception Island, a collapsed volcanic caldera containing an abandoned whaling station. I was the only person in a single kayak while everyone else paddled doubles. The scenery was spectacular and we paddled amongst seals, penguins and dramatic volcanic cliffs. Unfortunately things soon became chaotic as strong winds funnelled through the narrow entrance to the caldera. Plans changed, kayaks drifted towards cliffs and eventually one capsized completely. Thankfully nobody was hurt, but it was certainly not the smooth excursion anyone had planned. Despite that, I couldn't help smiling. Against all odds I'd finally paddled in Antarctica and completed a goal that had quietly been building for years. Better still, because the trip had gone so badly, nobody was charged the additional kayaking fee.

I spent the rest of the afternoon exploring Deception Island. The abandoned whaling station was fascinating from an engineering perspective and looked like the rustiest collection of metal I'd ever seen. It was also interesting imagining the people who lived and worked there in such a remote place. I chatted with Will and Ab about the history of the area and was one of the last people back to the ship. By then I felt like I'd squeezed every possible drop from the day.

That evening we did the polar plunge. A zodiac was positioned beside the ship and participants shuffled forward one by one with safety ropes tied around their waists. It felt a bit like walking to the gallows. The water was freezing but honestly not quite as bad as I'd expected. Afterwards we were handed bathrobes, a shot of vodka and directed towards the sauna and jacuzzis. Knowing I'd probably never return to Antarctica, I asked if I could jump again and somehow became the only person who did. The second vodka definitely helped. Watching others leap into Antarctic water while sitting in a jacuzzi beneath the stars was a fitting way to end the trip.

## Journey Home

The return crossing of the Drake Passage was much smoother. I skipped the seasickness tablets and felt fine, spending most of the time chatting with Tommy, watching lectures and learning far more geology than I ever expected. We had talks about life on Antarctic research stations, women in Antarctica and a final Q&A with the expedition team. As we entered the Beagle Channel and saw land again, it felt like the trip was coming to an end all too quickly.

One of the highlights of the return journey was the Seven Continents Club ceremony. Of the hundred marathon starters, eighty-one completed the race and sixteen of us received our medals. Holding it felt special. A journey that had taken years was finally complete. The race winner produced an incredible performance but I was equally impressed watching John collect second place at nearly seventy years of age. We took photos, celebrated together and enjoyed one final evening in Ushuaia before heading our separate ways. Most people flew home the following day while I returned north to continue travelling through South America.

## Afterthoughts

This trip exceeded every expectation I had. Antarctica itself was incredible, from the wildlife and landscapes to simply standing somewhere so remote. The marathon was unlike anything I've ever done and kayaking the seventh continent made it even more special. Yet when I look back, my strongest memories aren't necessarily of glaciers, penguins or finish lines.

They're of the people.

John and his remarkable running career. Tommy and our endless conversations. Nic, whose curiosity about my story eventually became a Runner's World article. Karen living her best life in Thailand. Nathan covered head to toe in mud. The expedition staff who somehow managed to make days of delays and rough seas just as enjoyable as Antarctica itself.

The marathon brought us together, but the people made the trip. Running a marathon on my seventh continent was a fantastic achievement and completing the Seven Continents Club felt like the perfect ending to a journey that started years earlier. If I had to choose one thing I'll remember most though, it will be the feeling of standing at the end of the world surrounded by extraordinary people, knowing we'd all shared something truly unique.
