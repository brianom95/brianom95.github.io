## Background

Located in the heart of the Principal Cordillera of the Andes in Argentina, Aconcagua is the highest point in both the Western and Southern Hemispheres. Standing at **6,961m**, it is the "Second Summit" of the world and the highest peak outside of Asia. Unlike the solitary rise of Kilimanjaro from the plains, Aconcagua is the centerpiece of a vast, rugged provincial park, situated roughly 110km from the city of Mendoza and just 15km from the Chilean border. Its name is believed to derive from the Quechua word *Akunkawa*, meaning "Stone Sentinel."

For centuries, the mountain held deep spiritual significance for the Inca Empire. In 1985, the discovery of a frozen child mummy at 5,300m proved that the Incas were performing high-altitude sacrifices on its slopes long before modern mountaineering began. Despite this ancient history, the first recorded European ascent didn't occur until 1897, when Matthias Zurbriggen reached the summit alone. Today, the peak is a magnet for those seeking to test themselves against extreme altitude without the requirement of technical vertical climbing.

While often labeled a "non-technical" mountain via its Normal Route, Aconcagua is far from a simple hike. The expedition typically takes **18 to 21 days**, a grueling timeframe compared to Kilimanjaro’s week-long trek. The challenges are formidable: thinning air with roughly 40% of the oxygen found at sea level, and the notorious *Viento Blanco* (White Wind), which can bring gusts exceeding 100km/h and temperatures as low as -30°C. While a 10K runner’s fitness is a baseline, this mountain demands a level of "heavy-load" endurance and mental grit required to survive weeks in a high-pressure, high-altitude wilderness.

The environment of Aconcagua is defined by its stark, desert-like beauty and extreme aridity. The **Lower Valleys** are characterized by dry, dusty paths and hardy shrubs, where mule trains provide the primary means of transport. Higher up, the **Intermediate Slopes** consist of vast scree fields and the famous *penitentes*—tall, blade-like snow formations created by the intense sun and dry winds. Finally, the **High Altitude Zone** is a world of rock and permanent ice, where the "Canaleta"—a steep, loose couloir just below the summit—serves as the final, exhausting gatekeeper to the "Colossus of the Americas."

## Acclimatisation is Key

At nearly 7,000m, Aconcagua sits on the edge of what the human body can endure for any significant length of time. The air here is profoundly thin; each breath contains only about 40% of the oxygen molecules available at sea level. While the biology of adaptation—the surge in red blood cell production and increased heart rate—is the same as on Kilimanjaro, the scale of the challenge is entirely different. On "The Stone Sentinel," acclimatisation is not just a strategy; it is the entire focus of the first two weeks.

To survive at this altitude, mountaineers use a "step" or "rotation" system. This involves carrying loads of food and gear to a higher camp, then descending to a lower altitude to sleep. This "climb high, sleep low" method triggers the body to produce EPO naturally, thickening the blood to carry more oxygen while allowing the heart and lungs to recover in the denser air below. Unlike the linear path of Kilimanjaro, an Aconcagua expedition is a series of loops, slowly inching your "ceiling" higher until the body is prepared for the final summit push.

The physical toll of this process is immense. Because the expedition lasts three weeks, the body begins to deteriorate even as it adapts. Muscle loss is common, and the extremely dry air leads to the "Andean cough," a persistent, raspy irritation of the lungs. At these heights, recovery is exceptionally slow; a simple headache or a night of poor sleep at Camp 2 (approx. 5,500m) can take days to resolve. You are constantly balancing the need to move upward against the risk of High Altitude Cerebral or Pulmonary Edema (HACE and HAPE), which are significantly more common here than on shorter African treks.

The final thousand meters of Aconcagua exist in a realm where the body is essentially dying, just at a slow enough rate to reach the top and get back down. While many climbers on Kilimanjaro might use Diamox to ease the transition, on Aconcagua, it is often viewed as a safety net rather than a shortcut. Success here isn't measured by how fast you can run a 10K, but by how well you can manage your hydration, your pacing, and your patience over twenty grueling days in a low-oxygen environment.

## Preparation & Planning

The logistics of Aconcagua begin in the city of Mendoza, Argentina. Unlike the straightforward park fees of Tanzania, every climber must visit the Provincial Park office in person to obtain their permit. This involves a bureaucratic dance of paying fees at a local bank, then returning with receipts to have your permit issued. The price fluctuates based on the season and your nationality, but it’s a non-negotiable first step. Mendoza itself is a world-class wine capital, and most climbers spend their final days of "civilization" here, gorging on steak and Malbec before the forced austerity of the mountain.

Packing for the Andes is a far more serious undertaking than the gear list for Africa. Because you are moving between 30°C heat in the valleys and -30°C on the summit, the "layering system" is pushed to its absolute limit. I traded my trail runners for triple-insulated mountaineering boots and my standard hiking jacket for a massive down "summit parka." We also had to source specialized gear like plastic double boots for the cold, crampons, and ice axes. While you can rent gear in Mendoza, the stakes are higher; a failed zipper or a poorly fitting boot at 6,000m isn't just an inconvenience—it’s an expedition-ender.

One of the most unique aspects of Aconcagua logistics is the use of mules. To get three weeks' worth of food, fuel, and high-altitude gear to Base Camp (Plaza de Mulas), we utilized a logistics provider. Each climber is typically allowed 60kg of gear to be carried by these hardy animals. You hike the first three days with only a light daypack, while the "Arrieros" (mule drivers) navigate the dusty, narrow trails with the heavy loads. Once you reach Base Camp, however, the "glamping" ends. From that point on, you are the porter, carrying your own tents, stoves, and food up to the high camps in a series of grueling back-and-forth carries.

On the medical front, the preparation is similar to Kili but extended. We packed a robust supply of Diamox, but also added Dexamethasone and Nifedipine—emergency drugs used to treat brain and lung swelling at extreme altitudes. Hydration becomes a full-time job; the air in the Andes is so dry that you lose significant moisture just by breathing. We planned for 5 to 6 liters of water a day, which meant hours spent melting snow on small gas stoves once we moved above the glacier lines.

Finally, there is the mental planning. You have to prepare for "waiting." On Kilimanjaro, you move every day. On Aconcagua, you might spend four days sitting in a tent at 5,000m waiting for a window of clear weather. I packed solar chargers, extra podcasts, and a deck of cards, knowing that the "Stone Sentinel" often requires as much patience as it does physical strength. We synchronized our calendars for a 20-day window, praying that the notorious *Viento Blanco* would stay calm long enough for us to make our move.

## Background

Aconcagua stands at 6,961 metres in the Andes of Argentina and is the highest mountain outside of Asia. Often referred to as a “trekking peak at altitude,” it is still a serious high-altitude objective, where weather, wind, and acclimatisation play a decisive role.

For me, it was a step up in both altitude and commitment after Kilimanjaro — a place where the margin for error becomes thinner, and patience becomes the most important piece of gear you carry.


## The Approach

The approach through the Horcones Valley is long, dry, and exposed. Unlike Kilimanjaro, there is no lush forest or gradual ecological transition — just rock, dust, and vast empty space framed by towering Andean peaks.

Base Camp (Plaza de Mulas) feels more like a temporary high-altitude city than a wilderness camp, with climbers from all over the world prepare for summit rotations.


## Acclimatisation and Weather

Acclimatisation on Aconcagua is slow and deliberate. The body has to adjust carefully to nearly 7,000 metres, and the rotation system between camps becomes a test of patience as much as fitness.

The mountain is defined by wind. Even on otherwise clear days, gusts can sweep across the upper mountain with force that makes progress slow and mentally draining.


## The Summit Push

Summit day begins in darkness and cold. The route is long, steady, and relentlessly upward. There are no technical climbing sections in most normal routes, but the altitude makes every step deliberate.

Above 6,500 metres, the air becomes noticeably thinner, and progress is measured in pauses as much as steps. The final ridge feels endless — a narrow line between exhaustion and focus.


## The Summit

Standing on the summit of Aconcagua, you are looking out over the spine of the Andes and a continent stretching away in every direction. It is quiet in a way that is difficult to describe — not just absence of sound, but absence of urgency.

It is not a dramatic summit in the Alpine sense. It is vast, exposed, and uncompromising. And that is what makes it memorable.


## Descent

On Aconcagua, the descent is not just a return — it is part of the challenge. Fatigue, altitude, and weather all compound on the way down.

By the time you return to base camp, the mountain feels both close and distant, as if the experience belongs to a slightly different version of yourself.


## Reflections

Aconcagua is not a technical climb, but it is a serious high-altitude mountain. It rewards patience, preparation, and humility more than strength or speed.

Compared to Kilimanjaro, it feels less like an introduction to altitude and more like a confirmation of what altitude actually demands: respect, restraint, and time.
