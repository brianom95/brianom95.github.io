## Introduction

Aconcagua stands at 6,961 metres in the Andes of Argentina and is the highest mountain outside of Asia. Often referred to as a “trekking peak at altitude,” it is still a serious high-altitude objective, where weather, wind, and acclimatisation play a decisive role.

For me, it was a step up in both altitude and commitment after Kilimanjaro — a place where the margin for error becomes thinner, and patience becomes the most important piece of gear you carry.


## The Approach

The approach through the Horcones Valley is long, dry, and exposed. Unlike Kilimanjaro, there is no lush forest or gradual ecological transition — just rock, dust, and vast empty space framed by towering Andean peaks.

Base Camp (Plaza de Mulas) feels more like a temporary high-altitude city than a wilderness camp, with climbers from all over the world preparing for summit rotations.


## Acclimatisation and Weather

Acclimatisation on Aconcagua is slow and deliberate. The body has to adjust carefully to nearly 7,000 metres, and the rotation system between camps becomes a test of patience as much as fitness.

The mountain is defined by wind. Even on otherwise clear days, gusts can sweep across the upper mountain with force that makes progress slow and mentally draining.


## The Summit Push

Summit day begins in darkness and cold. The route is long, steady, and relentlessly upward. There are no technical climbing sections in most normal routes, but the altitude makes every step deliberate.

Above 6,500 metres, the air becomes noticeably thinner, and progress is measured in pauses as much as steps. The final ridge feels endless — a narrow line between exhaustion and focus.


## The Summit

Standing on the summit of Aconcagua, you are looking out over the spine of the Andes and a continent stretching away in every direction. It is quiet in a way that is difficult to describe — not just absence of sound, but absence of urgency.

It is not a dramatic summit in the Alpine sense. It is vast, exposed, and uncompromising. And that is what makes it memorable.


## Descent

On Aconcagua, the descent is not just a return — it is part of the challenge. Fatigue, altitude, and weather all compound on the way down.

By the time you return to base camp, the mountain feels both close and distant, as if the experience belongs to a slightly different version of yourself.


## Reflections

Aconcagua is not a technical climb, but it is a serious high-altitude mountain. It rewards patience, preparation, and humility more than strength or speed.

Compared to Kilimanjaro, it feels less like an introduction to altitude and more like a confirmation of what altitude actually demands: respect, restraint, and time.
