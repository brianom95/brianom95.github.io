Patagonia has a mythical look to it. Endless grassy plains between turquoise rivers, snowy peaks, ancient glaciers, and a never-ending wind. Torres Del Paine in Chile is the perfect example of Patagonia, known for its tall granite pillars. The hiking route around the national park is often called the best long distance trek in the world, and after hiking it I can see why.

The W-Trek exploded in popularity over the last decade because of social media. Most people bus to the park, ferry across the lake, and spend 3-4 days hiking the front side of the range in a 'W' shape. The O-Trek instead completes a full loop, adding quieter valleys, forests, glaciers and the John Gardner Pass. It always appealed to me more.

I was six months into my travels through Central America & South America at the time. I had climbed [Aconcagua](aconcagua) two weeks earlier and was just about recovered. I had been reunited with my tent, and was excited for some time alone in wilderness. Patagonia was perfect for this as it feels truly remote. Weather systems roll in from Antarctica, trails disappear into huge valleys, and the mountains still feel wild. My experience of the O-Trek was truly unforgettable.

## Preparation & Planning

Booking the O-Trek is far more awkward than it should be. The campsites are split between two companies; Vertice manage the quieter back side of the loop, while Las Torres manage the busier W-Trek side. Vertice was straightforward and reasonably priced ($15), while Las Torres felt chaotic and expensive ($75+).

Like most people, I had to shape my itinerary around what bookings remained. Everything on the W-Trek was sold out six months before, but luckily I got one cancellation (Cuernos) and booked my other nights on the back side (Dickson, Perros & Paine Grande). My plan was five days of hiking; three for the quieter back side of the loop, and two for the W-Trek, costing $120 total. I booked my park permit online for $45 too.

The system feels broken. Bookings are held by tour companies and resellers who push people towards overpriced glamping setups. I met hikers paying thousands for this trip, and people who were forced to book some nights in pre-pitched tents ($250+), only to arrive and find the campsite is actually empty. As you'd guess, there are no refunds.

At the other extreme, I met people who setup at campsites without bookings and never paid. You'd need one fake booking to show at the ranger station between Séron & Dickson, but after that there’s barely any signal and nobody checks. Some people show up to each reception and pay there, making the whole booking system feel redundant. I was glad to have everything sorted beforehand, but one missing booking shouldn't be a deal breaker.

## Arrival in Puerto Natales

I flew from Mendoza to Puerto Montt, then took a bus to Puerto Natales. I stayed in Last Hope hostel where every converstaion revolves around weather forecasts and campsite bookings, though almost no one was doing the full O-Trek. The town is full of hikers and outdoor gear shops so I felt right at home. The wind brought a chill that had me wearing all my layers, but the port looked really pretty. 

In town I finalised my food for the hike which was mainly porridge, cous-cous, noodles, wraps, salami and every kind of biscuit. I was cold soaking my meals (thank you Aisling for the new peanut butter jar), and there was usually hot water at each campsite anyway. I booked a return bus ticket to the national park for $24, had one of the best meals of my life El Brisket, and after a few chats in what was a lovely hostel I got an early night.

## Day 1
#### Las Torres to Dickson
*Distance: 32.4km | Ascent: 800m*

After a 6:00am breakfast I caught the bus into Torres Del Paine where I met Thomas from Paris. We started hiking together from the park entrance beneath clear skies and perfect views of the Towers. We debated hiking them immediately, but both decided to save that until the end.

The first section was fast and mostly flat. We stopped for lunch at Serón before continuing into stronger winds. Patagonia’s reputation for wind is completely deserved. Gusts would suddenly hit hard enough to stop you walking properly.

Later I caught up with Flora from the UK who had already hiked the Towers yesterday. Apparently bad weather often blocks the view, so she planned to attempt them at both the start and end of her trip just to be safe.

The scenery became greener towards Dickson with rivers braiding through huge valleys beneath snowy peaks. I reached camp roughly six hours after leaving the entrance and instantly loved the place. A huge river flowed beside camp which looked perfect for packrafting.

I pitched early, journaled for a while and unsuccessfully tried removing an annoying splinter I’d picked up from grabbing grass while walking. Most people arrived carrying enormous backpacks which always reassures me my own setup is probably fine.

That evening we ate together, walked down to the river beach and bought chocolate cake from the campsite shop. Patagonia hiking calories don’t count anyway.

## Day 2
#### Dickson to Perros
*Distance: 14km | Ascent: 540m*

Today was intentionally short. Flora and I hiked together through a narrow valley with forest trails high above a roaring river. Wooden bridges crossed rapids and waterfalls while the scenery steadily became wilder.

Eventually we reached a lake so windy we could barely stand upright near the shore. Even when the weather is good, Patagonia still feels hostile somehow.

Los Perros campsite sits nicely sheltered amongst trees and everyone there seemed nervous about tomorrow. Rangers repeatedly warned hikers they must leave before 7:00am or they wouldn’t be allowed over John Gardner Pass. Online discussions make the pass sound terrifying too, so camp had this strange atmosphere where everyone packed gear and discussed forecasts like a major expedition was ahead.

I never wanted to stay at Los Perros because it makes for such a short day, but bookings dictated the itinerary. That evening I sat in my tent eating snacks from the shop and watching downloaded movies while the wind battered the trees outside.

## Day 3
#### Perros to Paine Grande
*Distance: 27.7km | Ascent: 1,250m*

People started walking past my tent from 4:00am onwards. By 6:30am campsite staff were practically shouting at hikers to leave immediately or risk being turned around. I eventually left around 7:00am wondering what exactly waited above us.

Honestly, not much.

Within forty five minutes I’d overtaken the furthest ahead hikers including a nice Canadian man who also seemed confused by the panic. The climb itself was beautiful; windy, icy and with some snow, but very manageable. I reached John Gardner Pass after roughly 1hr 15mins and the view over Grey Glacier was unbelievable.

The glacier stretches endlessly through the valley while huge blue walls of ice disappear into the distance. Two hours after leaving camp I passed the ranger hut on the far side; the same checkpoint people were convinced they might not reach by 2:00pm.

The descent was steep and slippy in places but nowhere near as dramatic as people make out. Eventually the trail levelled beside the glacier with long suspension bridges swaying in the wind above bright blue water and floating icebergs.

At the final bridge I randomly met Guillermo from Andorra who I’d previously met months earlier while cycling Bolivia’s Death Road. He was the first person I’d seen in hours.

I reached Grey Campsite by 10:00am, dried my tent in the sun, had lunch and continued onwards. The glacier views somehow kept getting better while waterfalls and rainbows filled the opposite cliffs.

Later I met Tylar & Faith from the US after recognising Tylar’s Durston Kakwa backpack. Backpack nerds always find each other eventually. We walked together towards Paine Grande where pitching tents in the wind became a battle.

That evening I had pizza and beers with Rachel, Devon & Sonna, who had also summited Aconcagua just days after me two weeks earlier. Strong winds were forecast again tomorrow so I planned another early start.

## Day 4
#### Paine Grande to Los Cuernos
*Distance: 26.5km | Ascent: 1,020m*

The rain finally stopped before dawn and I left camp quickly. Two hours later I reached Italiano campsite where everyone leaves their backpacks before hiking into the French Valley.

I drank loads of water and continued upwards carrying almost nothing. The valley is spectacular with huge rocky slides, hanging glaciers and steep cliffs. The final climb became a bit of a slog but the views were worth it.

Wind intensified badly near the top so I trail ran sections of the descent. Back at Italiano I discovered why the trail had felt so quiet all morning; rangers had closed the route at 8:00am due to wind and more than a hundred hikers were waiting below hoping it might reopen.

Somehow I’d slipped through just before closure despite having almost no flexibility in my itinerary. I couldn’t believe my luck.

After grabbing my backpack I continued onwards. One hour later I reached Francés campsite where most of the group from yesterday were staying, then continued along the lake towards Cuernos; the only W-Trek booking I’d managed to get.

The campsite sits on a steep slope with tents pitched on wooden platforms. At check-in they literally hand you a power drill so you can screw clips into the platform to tie down your tent. It’s a funny experience.

I spent the evening drying gear, journalling, eating another cold soaked meal and watching movies in my tent while rain hammered the lake outside.

## Day 5
#### Los Cuernos to Las Torres
*Distance: 12.8km | Ascent: 440m*

The wind howled all night. By morning reception informed us that both out-and-back hikes were closed for the entire day, including the Towers. Missing the main highlight of the whole trip on the final day would’ve been brutal.

All campsites were supposedly fully booked despite most being half empty, so I decided to quietly camp near the park entrance and attempt the Towers at sunrise the next morning instead. It meant sacrificing my hostel booking and missing my return bus, but future me could deal with that later.

The walk itself was beautiful. Suspension bridges crossed huge rivers while snowy peaks towered above the valley. Eventually I reached the day-hiker crowds near the Towers trailhead which felt chaotic compared to the quiet back side of the O-Trek.

I jogged the final downhill into camp where there were surprisingly few tents for a “sold out” campsite. I found a discreet spot away from reception cameras and quietly pitched my tent hoping nobody would notice.

Thankfully nobody did. I spent the night eating empanadas and Pringles while rain hammered the tent outside.

## Day 6
#### The Towers
*Distance: 21.5km | Ascent: 1,280m*

My alarm went at 4:00am beneath a perfectly clear sky filled with stars. I left most of my gear behind and started climbing through almost empty trails.

Around 6:00am snow began covering the ground and the route became slightly confusing where ice buried the trail markers. There was also a hilarious warning sign featuring the funniest “no pooing” symbol I’ve ever seen.

I reached the viewpoint while it was still dark and waited beside the frozen lake for sunrise. Torres Del Paine absolutely deserves the hype. The towers slowly glow orange while the lake reflects the changing light beneath them. It was easily the highlight of the whole trek and completely justified the extra day.

After sunrise I practically ran back down hoping to catch the first bus. Along the way I met Thomas again, the UK couple and a nice guy from Taiwan.

Then roughly 500m from the end I saw two people photographing a bush and assumed there was probably a small bird hiding inside.

It was a puma.

The animal casually walked within five metres of me before disappearing back into the grass. Pumas are the symbol of Torres Del Paine and incredibly rare to see. Ending the trip with that felt absurdly lucky.

I missed the first bus anyway, so with time to spare I bought the O-Trek badge and sewed it onto my jacket while drying gear in the sun. The return journey became chaotic with every bus full and nobody accepting yesterday’s ticket, but eventually I made it back to the comfort of Last Hope Hostel.

Over the next few days almost everyone I’d met on trail gradually returned to town. We reunited for another incredible dinner at El Brisket followed by far too many drinks afterwards. As always, it’s the people.

## Afterthoughts

This trail genuinely might deserve its reputation as the best hike in the world. Every day feels cinematic. Glaciers collapse into lakes, rivers glow turquoise beneath suspension bridges and the mountains constantly shift between sunshine, rain and snow within the same hour.

The biggest downside is how commercialised the booking system has become. Campsites which could fit hundreds more tents somehow sell out almost a year in advance while tour companies and resellers hold huge blocks of bookings for overpriced glamping setups.

The difference between the two campsite companies is also very noticeable. Vertice, who run the quieter back side of the circuit, were far easier to deal with and considerably cheaper. The Las Torres side near the W-Trek felt much more crowded and commercial.

That said, the actual hiking is fantastic. The O-Trek makes far more sense to me than only doing the W-Trek. Hiking a full loop around the massif feels like a proper journey, and the quieter northern side has some of the best scenery on the entire route.

Despite the intimidating reputation, the trail itself is very approachable for anyone reasonably fit. The dramatic warnings around John Gardner Pass are mostly because the route attracts lots of inexperienced hikers. In good conditions there is nothing particularly technical about it.

The campsites are also incredibly scenic and surprisingly sociable. Dickson especially is one of the nicest campsites I’ve ever stayed in. Shops, bars and restaurants throughout the route remove the risk of people underpacking food and create a nice atmosphere every evening.

Financially, this hike can vary wildly. I completed the entire O-Trek for roughly $180 by carrying my own tent and staying flexible with bookings. Many people spend far more. I met one girl who had paid nearly $3,000 for her trip.

Still, none of that changes the fact that this place is extraordinary. Torres Del Paine completely lived up to every expectation I had for it. The weather, the glaciers, the wildlife and the scale of the mountains all somehow felt even better than expected.

I loved every second of it.
