Patagonia has a mythical look to it. Endless grassy plains between turquoise rivers, big snowy peaks, ancient glaciers, and a never-ending wind. Torres Del Paine National Park in Chile is the perfect example of Patagonia, known for its tall granite pillars. The hiking route around the park is often called the best long distance trek in the world, which I have to agree with.

The W-Trek exploded in popularity over the last decade because of social media. Most people bus to the park, ferry across the lake, and spend 3-4 days hiking the front side of the mountain range in a 'W' shape. The O-Trek instead completes a full loop over 7-8 days, adding quieter valleys, forests, glaciers and the John Gardner Pass. The O-Trek is the adventurous option and always appealed to me more.

I was six months into my travels through Central America & South America at the time. I had climbed Aconcagua two weeks earlier and was just about recovered. I had been reunited with my tent, and was excited for some time in wilderness. Patagonia was perfect for this as it feels truly remote. Weather systems roll in from Antarctica, trails disappear into huge valleys, and the mountains still feel wild. My experience of the O-Trek was unforgettable.

## Preparation & Planning

Booking the O-Trek is far more awkward than it should be. The campsites are split between two companies, Vertice managing the quieter back section, and Las Torres managing the busier W-Trek at the front. Vertice was mostly straightforward and reasonably priced ($15), while Las Torres was chaotic and expensive ($75).

Like most people, I had to shape my itinerary around what bookings remained. Everything on the W-Trek was sold out six months before, but luckily I got one cancellation (Cuernos) and booked my other nights on the back side (Dickson, Perros & Paine Grande). My plan was five days of hiking; three for the quieter back side of the loop, and two for the W-Trek. I booked my park permit online too ($45).

The system is really annoying. Bookings are held by tour companies and resellers who push people towards overpriced glamping setups. I met hikers paying thousands for this trip, and people who were forced to book some of their nights in pre-pitched tents ($250), only to arrive and find the campsite is actually empty. As you'd guess, there are no refunds.

On the other end of the scale I met people who paid nothing, just setup at the campsites and never paid. You'd need one fake booking to show at the ranger station between Séron & Dickson, and after that no one will ever care. Some people will arrive to campsites with no booking and pay there even, making the whole rigged booking system feel pointless. I was happy to have my bookings sorted

. I also met people who paid nothing and just didn't pay for any campsites. Some will say you can show up to campsites and pay in cash, and most are admittedly empty, so part of it's down to how well you'd deal wioth the stress of that. By hiking fast managed the full circuit for roughly $180.

Getting there is simple enough. Most people stay in Puerto Natales beforehand, then take one of the early morning buses into the national park. The buses are timed around the ferry schedules and trail regulations so nearly everyone starts at the same time each morning.

I was already travelling through South America so my preparation mostly involved changing gear after Aconcagua. I swapped my small travel backpack for my hiking rucksack, adding my tent, sleeping bag and sleeping mat. To save weight I cold soaked my meals instead of carrying a stove, using a peanut butter jar that Aisling had thankfully convinced me to keep. It worked surprisingly well, especially with campsite hot water taps helping soften cous cous and noodles.

## Arrival in Puerto Natales

I flew from Mendoza to Puerto Montt early that morning before continuing south by bus to Puerto Natales. The further south you travel in Patagonia, the more dramatic the landscape becomes. Endless empty plains stretched towards snowy mountains while the wind rocked the bus for hours.

Puerto Natales instantly felt like a hiking town. Almost everyone carried trekking poles or giant rucksacks and every second shop seemed to sell outdoor gear. I checked into Last Hope Hostel, which sits directly beside the bus station and is probably the centre of the hiking world during summer. Every common room conversation revolved around weather forecasts, campsite bookings and whether people were doing the W or the O.

Almost nobody I met was hiking the full O-Trek. Most were doing the shorter W-Trek and seemed slightly horrified by the idea of carrying a tent around the full loop. I spent the afternoon exploring town, buying food supplies and looking through some excellent outdoor shops. Patagonia has a strange obsession with miniature salami packs and after trying them once I understood why. They became a staple food for the next week.

That evening I ate in El Brisket which I can confidently say is one of the best post-hike meals I’ve ever had. Incredible food, huge portions and exactly what you want before living on wraps and cous cous for a week. Afterwards I bought my 7:00am return bus ticket to the park and headed to bed early while the Patagonian wind howled outside the hostel windows.

## Day 1
#### Las Torres to Dickson
*Distance: 32.4km | Ascent: 800m*

Breakfast was at 6:00am and by 7:00am we were on the bus heading towards Torres Del Paine. I met Thomas from Paris during the journey and we ended up hiking together from the entrance. The weather looked unbelievably clear and you could already see the famous towers in the distance glowing above the valley. We debated hiking up to them immediately, but both decided to save that out-and-back for the end of the loop.

The first section was fast and mostly flat. We reached Serón campsite quickly and stopped for lunch before continuing onwards into stronger winds. Patagonia’s reputation for wind is not exaggerated. Gusts would suddenly hit with enough force to stop you walking properly and at exposed sections you’d instinctively lean sideways to stay upright.

After the next pass I naturally drifted ahead and later caught up with Flora, a girl from the UK who had arrived the previous day. She had already hiked to the Towers and camped at Serón. Apparently bad weather and cloud regularly obscure the peaks so she had cleverly planned to attempt the Towers at both the start and end of her trip to maximise her chances. Yesterday had been perfectly clear though, so one attempt was probably enough.

The landscape became greener as we approached Dickson. Rivers braided through huge valleys and snowy peaks appeared behind the campsite. I reached Dickson roughly six hours after leaving the park entrance and immediately loved the place. It’s one of the nicest campsites I’ve ever stayed in. A huge river flowed beside camp and looked perfect for packrafting. Watching it twist away into the valley made me wish I’d somehow brought a boat instead of a tent.

I pitched early, journaled for a while and tried unsuccessfully to remove an annoying splinter I’d picked up from grabbing grass while walking. One blade had somehow buried itself beneath the skin of my finger and irritated me all evening.

Most people arrived carrying absolutely enormous backpacks. One of the strange comforts of well established trails is seeing how much unnecessary gear people bring. It reassures you that your own lightweight setup is probably fine.

I made wraps for dinner while watching strange birds wander through camp and later used the campsites free hot water to soften my cous cous. Thomas, Flora and a few others all ate together before walking down to a beach beside the river. The campsite shop sold chocolate cake which felt impossible to refuse. Patagonia hiking calories don’t count anyway.

## Day 2
#### Dickson to Perros
*Distance: 14km | Ascent: 540m*

Today was intentionally short. The route followed a narrow valley through beautiful forest trails high above a roaring river. Flora and I hiked together most of the day, moving at a similar pace while crossing wooden bridges over rapids and waterfalls.

The scenery steadily became wilder. Huge glacial rivers crashed through the valley and eventually opened beside a lake so windy we could barely stand upright near the shore. Patagonia constantly feels on the edge of chaos. Even sunny weather somehow still feels dramatic.

Los Perros campsite sits nicely sheltered amongst trees and everyone there seemed tense about tomorrow. Rangers and campsite staff repeatedly warned people they must leave before 7:00am or they wouldn’t be allowed over John Gardner Pass. They stressed how long and difficult the following day was supposed to be and spent the evening reminding nervous hikers about cut-off times.

I hadn’t wanted to stay at Los Perros in the first place because it makes for such a short day, but bookings dictated the itinerary. Everything online made the pass sound terrifying too, with people speaking about it as though it were some Himalayan expedition.

The atmosphere in camp reflected that. People cooked early dinners, discussed weather forecasts and packed gear with visible anxiety. Meanwhile I sat in my tent eating snacks from the shop and watching downloaded movies while the wind battered the trees outside.

## Day 3
#### Perros to Paine Grande
*Distance: 27.7km | Ascent: 1,250m*

People started walking past my tent from 4:00am onwards. It sounded like an evacuation. Everyones fear of the pass had created this strange panic where hikers believed they needed to leave in complete darkness to survive the day.

By 6:30am the campsite staff were practically shouting at people to leave immediately or risk being turned around. I packed calmly and finally set off around 7:00am wondering what exactly waited above us.

Honestly? Not much.

Within forty five minutes I’d overtaken the furthest ahead hikers including a very nice Canadian man who seemed relieved someone else also thought the panic was excessive. The climb itself was beautiful. Windy, icy and with patches of snow, but entirely manageable. I reached the top of John Gardner Pass after roughly 1hr 15mins and the view down onto Grey Glacier was absolutely unreal.

The glacier stretches endlessly through the valley like a frozen river disappearing into the horizon. Huge walls of blue ice cracked beneath the clouds while the wind whipped snow across the pass. It was one of the best views of the entire trip.

Two hours after leaving camp I passed the ranger hut on the far side; the same checkpoint they insisted people must reach before 2:00pm or turn around. I had the trail completely to myself now and loved every second of it.

The descent was steep and slippery in places but nowhere near as dangerous as online discussions make it sound. Eventually the trail levelled into rolling hills beside the glacier. Long suspension bridges swayed violently in the wind and below them massive chunks of ice floated through bright blue water.

At the final suspension bridge I unexpectedly met Guillermo from Andorra who I’d previously met months earlier while cycling Bolivia’s Death Road. He was the first person I’d seen in hours and we both laughed at the absurdity of randomly meeting again in Patagonia.

I reached Grey Campsite by 10:00am, meaning the entire pass had taken only three hours. The place was packed with W-Trek hikers eating breakfast and preparing for much shorter days. I dried my wet tent in the sun, had lunch and continued onwards.

The views somehow kept improving. Icebergs drifted through the lake while huge waterfalls poured down the opposite cliffs creating constant rainbows. Along this section I met Tylar and Faith from the US after recognising Tylar’s Durston Kakwa 40 backpack; a very high-end ultralight bag. Backpack nerds always find each other eventually.

We walked together towards Paine Grande where pitching tents in the wind became an expedition of its own. I found some shelter and later met Sonna who was camped beside me. Amazingly, she had also summited Aconcagua within days of me two weeks earlier.

That evening I had pizza and beers with Rachel, Devon and Sonna while everyone discussed forecasts of strong winds tomorrow afternoon. Given Patagonia’s obsession with trail closures, I decided another early start was probably wise.

## Day 4
#### Paine Grande to Los Cuernos
*Distance: 26.5km | Ascent: 1,020m*

The rain finally stopped sometime before dawn and I left camp quickly at 7:45am. Two hours later I reached Italiano campsite where hikers leave their large backpacks before climbing into the French Valley. Wooden shelves overflowed with bags while everyone reorganised gear for the out-and-back climb.

I drank as much water as possible and continued upwards carrying almost nothing. The valley is spectacular. Giant rocky slides cut down the mountainside and reminded me strangely of Norway. The final climb became a bit of a slog but the views towards the hanging glaciers and the dramatic Británico cliffs were worth it.

Wind intensified badly near the top and I ended up trail running sections of the descent to get back down quickly. When I returned to Italiano I finally understood why the trail had felt so quiet all morning. Rangers had closed the route at 8:00am due to wind and more than a hundred hikers were waiting below hoping it might reopen.

Based on the forecast and what staff were saying, it clearly wasn’t going to happen. Somehow I had slipped through just before closure despite having almost no flexibility in my itinerary. I couldn’t believe my luck.

After collecting my backpack I continued onwards. One hour later I reached Francés campsite where most of the group from yesterday were staying. From there it was a short coastal walk beside a rocky beach to Los Cuernos; the only booking I’d managed to secure on this side of the trail after grabbing a last minute cancellation.

The campsite itself is built onto a steep slope overlooking the lake. Tents are pitched on wooden platforms and during check-in they literally hand you a power drill so you can screw metal clips into the boards to secure your tent. It’s one of the strangest campsite experiences I’ve ever had.

I spent the afternoon drying gear, journalling and talking with a nice couple from the UK before rain arrived again that evening. Free hot water taps made another cold soaked meal surprisingly enjoyable and I spent the night in my tent eating snacks and watching movies while the storm rolled across the lake outside.

## Day 5
#### Los Cuernos to Las Torres
*Distance: 12.8km | Ascent: 440m*

The wind howled all night long. By morning reception informed us that both out-and-back routes were closed for the entire day. Terrible news for anyone still hoping to see the French Valley, but even worse for me because it meant the Towers hike was also closed.

This was supposed to be my final day and I suddenly faced the possibility of missing the main highlight entirely.

All campsites showed as fully booked months in advance despite many being visibly empty fields capable of holding hundreds more tents. I decided my best option was simply to hike back to the campsite near the park entrance, discreetly camp there without being noticed and attempt the Towers at sunrise the following morning.

It meant sacrificing a hostel night I’d already paid for and missing my return bus, but I figured future me could deal with that problem later.

The walk itself was beautiful. More suspension bridges crossed huge rivers while snowy peaks towered above the valleys. Fog gradually lifted and for the first time in days there was almost no wind at lower elevations.

Eventually I reached the final bridge near the Towers trailhead where the day-hiker crowds began appearing. Compared to the quiet back side of the O-Trek, this section felt chaotic. Hundreds of tourists wandered around the base area taking photos while shuttle buses arrived constantly.

I jogged the final downhill into camp where surprisingly few tents occupied this supposedly sold out campsite. There were cameras around reception so I found a discreet hidden spot away from the main area and quietly pitched my tent hoping nobody would notice.

It rained heavily all night while I sat inside eating empanadas and Pringles, watching movies and wondering whether security would appear and throw me out. Thankfully nobody ever did.

## Day 6
#### The Towers
*Distance: 21.5km | Ascent: 1,280m*

My alarm went at 4:00am beneath a perfectly clear sky filled with stars. I left everything unnecessary inside the tent and started climbing with only the essentials.

The trail was almost empty and I moved quickly through the darkness. Around 6:00am snow began covering the ground and the route became slightly confusing where ice obscured the trail markers. There was also a hilarious warning sign featuring perhaps the funniest “no pooing” symbol ever created.

The final climb became slippery but I reached the viewpoint while it was still completely dark. I found the best spot beside the lake and waited quietly as the first light appeared.

Sunrise at Torres Del Paine absolutely deserves the hype. The towers slowly glow orange while the frozen lake reflects the changing light beneath them. Everyone speaks in whispers as if the place somehow demands it. It was easily the highlight of the entire trek and completely justified the extra day.

After sunrise I practically ran back down hoping to catch the first bus out of the park. Along the way I met the UK couple again, Thomas, and a nice guy from Taiwan.

Then, about 500m from the end of the trail, I saw two people photographing a bush and assumed there was probably a small bird hiding inside.

It was a puma.

The animal casually walked within roughly five metres of me before disappearing back into the grass. Pumas are the symbol of Torres Del Paine and incredibly rare to see. After six days in Patagonia, somehow ending the trip with that felt absurdly lucky.

I missed the first bus anyway, so with an hour to spare at the entrance I bought the official O-Trek badge and sewed it onto my jacket while drying gear in the sun. The return journey became chaotic as every bus was full and nobody would honour yesterday’s ticket, but eventually I made it back to the comfort of Last Hope Hostel.

Over the next few days almost everyone I’d met on trail gradually returned to town. We reunited for another incredible dinner at El Brisket followed by far too many drinks afterwards. As always, the people become a huge part of these trips.

## Afterthoughts

This trail genuinely might deserve its reputation as the best hike in the world. Every day feels cinematic. Glaciers collapse into lakes, rivers glow turquoise beneath suspension bridges and the mountains constantly shift between sunshine, rain and snow within the same hour.

The biggest downside is how tightly managed and commercialised the entire area has become. The park infrastructure itself is excellent, but the booking system feels broken. Campsites which could fit hundreds more tents somehow “sell out” almost a year in advance while guiding companies and resellers seem to hold huge blocks of spots.

Many hikers end up forced into absurdly expensive glamping setups or pre-pitched tents costing hundreds of dollars. I met one couple who had booked a luxury tent because nothing else remained available, only to arrive and discover the campsite was practically empty. They asked to downgrade to a normal tent pitch since they already carried their own tent, but were refused.

The difference between the two campsite companies is also very noticeable. Vertice, who run the quieter back side of the circuit, were far easier to deal with and considerably cheaper. The Las Torres side near the W-Trek felt much more commercial and overcrowded.

That said, the actual hiking is fantastic. The O-Trek makes infinitely more sense to me than only doing the W-Trek. Hiking a full loop around the massif feels like a proper journey. The quieter northern side has some of the best scenery on the entire route and should absolutely not be skipped.

Despite the intimidating reputation, the trail itself is very approachable for anyone reasonably fit. The dramatic warnings around John Gardner Pass are mostly because the route attracts lots of inexperienced hikers. In good conditions there is nothing particularly technical about it.

The campsites are also incredibly scenic and surprisingly sociable. Dickson especially is one of the nicest campsites I’ve ever seen. Shops, bars and restaurants throughout the route mean people don’t need to carry excessive food and there’s a nice communal atmosphere every evening.

Financially, this hike can vary wildly. I completed the entire O-Trek for roughly $180 by carrying my own tent and remaining flexible with bookings. Many people spend far more. I met one girl who had paid nearly $3,000 for her trip. If you require fixed dates, pre-booked accommodation or fully catered options then Patagonia becomes expensive very quickly.

Still, none of that changes the fact that this place is extraordinary. Torres Del Paine completely lived up to every expectation I had for it. The weather, the glaciers, the wildlife, the scale of the mountains and even the chaos of the booking system somehow all became part of the experience.

I loved every second of it.
