My first marathon was the 2021 [Killarney Ironman](killarney-full-ironman), which gave me a confidence for difficult events, and a need for a new goal. I had plans to travel when pandemic restrictions eased, so I looked for something that would compliment that. I researched events in extreme locations like Antartica, and found the Seven Continents Club: the small group of people who have run a marathon on every continent. At the time more people had been to space, and ten times more people had summitted Everest.

The Antarctica Marathon began in 1995, sailing south on Soviet oil tankers to run a tough course along research station supply trails. Nowadays the event is more official, using expedition cruise ships and seeing more of the continent. There's an award ceremony on the ship for those having completed their seventh continent, so I knew Antarctica should be my final one. The race had a three year waiting list, so I started to plan which races I would do.

## 1. Europe
#### Rome, Italy
**27 March 2022**  
**04:23:53**

My first official marathon, and my introduction to big city racing. Rome was chaotic from the start, with cobbled streets, pacers, music and crowds wrapping around the Colosseum. I started too fast with the sub-4 group and held on longer than I should have, before the heat slowly broke the race apart. The final 10km turned into survival running in the Roman sun, relying heavily on aid stations and shade. It wasn’t pretty, but it was a perfect introduction to what marathon running actually demands.

## 2. North America
#### Vancouver, Canada
**01 May 2022**  
**04:27:18**

Vancouver felt controlled compared to Rome. A clean, fast course along the seawall and through the city, with long stretches where the pace settled naturally. I found rhythm early and held it through most of the race, with the city skyline and mountains constantly shifting beside me. It was the first time I felt like I was actually racing a marathon rather than just surviving one.

## 3. Oceania
#### Auckland, New Zealand
**30 October 2022**  
**04:45:27**

Auckland was defined by weather more than anything else. A storm rolled in overnight and never really left, turning the race into a battle with wind, rain and flooding roads. The harbour bridge section into the city was unforgettable, with the wind almost stopping you mid-stride. It was miserable at times, but strangely uplifting by the end, where even normal conditions would have felt like a gift.

## 4. Asia
#### Chiang Mai, Thailand
**18 December 2022**  
**04:56:49**

A night marathon through Chiang Mai’s ancient city walls, designed to avoid the heat but still brutally humid. The opening laps around the moat were magical, completely silent under floodlights, before the race drifted into long dark stretches where staying awake became part of the challenge. Pineapple, sunrise and steady pacing slowly brought me back into it, finishing as the city woke up around me.

## 5. Africa
#### Marrakech, Morocco
**29 June 2023**  
**04:45:26**

Marrakech was a mix of family, travel and racing through one of the busiest cities in Africa. I ran much of it with my dad, which made the early kilometres fly by, before the race spread out into the outskirts of the city and desert edges. The weather was perfect, but the race still turned into a grind in places, finished with a final push back towards the medina.

## 6. South America
#### Salento, Colombia
**03 November 2024**  
**07:40:25**

Easily the toughest marathon of the seven. Salento was more mountain expedition than road race, with over 3,000m of climbing, river crossings, and trails that barely resembled paths at all. Mud, altitude and steep descents made running almost secondary at times. It was brutal from the first kilometre, but the coffee region and cloud forest scenery made it unforgettable.

## 7. Antarctica
#### King George Island
**25 March 2025**  
**05:38:22**

The final continent and the reason the whole project had structure from the beginning. A three-year waiting list led to a slow build of races across the world, all timed to end here. Getting to Antarctica meant sailing the Drake Passage, waiting for weather windows, and hoping the marathon would even go ahead.

The race itself was unlike anything else: six laps between research stations, across mud, rock, sand and wind that never really stopped. You passed penguins, ice-cold water, and landscapes that felt completely detached from anywhere else on Earth. The finish line felt less like the end of a race and more like the closing of something much longer.
