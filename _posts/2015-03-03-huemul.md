It had been an adventurous few weeks on [Aconcagua](aconcagua) and the [O-Trek](otrek). Moving deeper into Patagonia brought me to my next big hike, the notorious Huemul Circuit in Argentina. This is often called Patagonia's most difficult hike. All I knew beforehand was the terrain is rough and slow, some parts are on a glacier, and big rivers are crossed by tyrolean traverses where you pull yourself across on a wire. That was enough to convince me.

## Introduction

The Huemul Circuit is a remote loop in Argentina’s Los Glaciares National Park, beginning and ending in El Chaltén. Most people hike it in four days, camping at Laguna Toro, Paso del Viento and Bahia de los Témpanos, though I combined the first two days to complete it in three. The route crosses rough scree slopes, muddy forests, glacier viewpoints and two tyrolean traverses over glacial rivers. 

What makes the hike famous is its proximity to the Southern Patagonian Ice Field; the third largest outside Antarctica and Greenland. Huge glaciers spill down into valleys filled with turquoise lakes and floating icebergs. Weather changes constantly and strong winds are challenging. Compared to the crowds around Fitz Roy (the most popular hike in Argentina, and the mountain on the Patagonia logo), very few people hike the Huemul Circuit. It still feels wild.

## Arrival in El Chaltén

I arrived late into El Chaltén by bus and found my hostel. Originally I planned to hike Fitz Roy the next day, but a huge storm was forecasted so I changed my plan. There was a perfect three day weather window if I started the next morning, so I combined the first two stages to hike the Huemul before the storm hit. The next morning I started with my errands.

Gear rental in Patagonia is unbelievably expensive, which made me glad I already had my own tent, sleeping bag and ground mat. I just needed to rent a climbing harness and carabiners for the tyrolean traverses. I priced every rental shop in town and the cheapest was still more than it would cost to buy one new at home, but beggers can't be choosers.

I stocked up on equally expensive food for the trail, which was mainly porridge, wraps with salami, and noodles I'd cold soak for dinners. Plus loads of cheese, nuts and snacks. Shops didn't open until 9:00am, so I was at the trail a little later than hoped at 10:00am.

Argentina had just introduced a new national park entry fee, costing around $30 at the time. Their card systems were offline when I arrived so everyone was waved through for free. For any other hikes in the area you can just enter the park from any of the countless trails that have no warden hut, or go before 9:00am when they start work. For the Huemul I did have to pass this entrance, so this was a lucky start.

## Day 1
#### El Chaltén to Paso del Viento
*Distance: 30.2km | Ascent: 1,620m*

Near the trailhead there’s a ranger hut where you fill out a form in case you go missing, then show proof before being allowed continue. He wished me luck and I headed up the valley, looking to the right at the huge granite towers wondering which was Fitz Roy.

The first section was fast and easy. Nice lake views beside the trail and snowy mountains on the horizon. I finished the Day 1 section in three hours and had lunch at the Laguna Toro campsite. The only people here were some climbers from Colorado, on their way down from a summit that morning.

From there the trail became rougher. Steep rocky climbs, loose scree and sections where the route became difficult to follow. Then came the first tyrolean traverse which was terrifying.

<p align="center" width="100%">
    <img width="400" src="/images/huemul1a.jpeg">
</p>

The rapid below is violent and deafening. I’d studied videos the night before on how to cross them properly, but I still doubted myself as I put my weight on it high above the water. Pulling yourself across is surprisingly exhausting as it's slightly uphill too. I was very relieved to reach the far side, and walked down to the rapid so I could look up at the wire.

It got far windier as I started the glacier section. The ice was littered with deep crevasses that made it too dangerous to walk on, so I made my way along scree on the left edge. The ice got bright blue and looked endless. I had a hard time navigating collapsed icy sections to get past it, but eventually was on thr far side climbing a pass beside a roaring river.

<p align="center" width="100%">
<img width="400" src="/images/huemul1b.jpeg">
</p>

The ground was scattered with bright, mineral covered rocks. On top of the pass were small alpine lakes, and a view of even more glaciers beyond. I descended on loose scree towards my campsite at Paso del Viento and tucked my tent between rocks on the lake edge. 

There were six other tents, all people trying to beat the same storm. A few swam in the freezing lake, while I filtered water from a nearby stream and chatted to my Australian neighbor. Rain that evening had us retreat to our tents, but I was happy to have a movie night with snacks. Something I had missed while travelling.

## Day 2
#### Paso del Viento to Bahia de los Témpanos
*Distance: 15.1km | Ascent: 530m*

Today looks short, but doesn't feel like it. Up and down scree slopes along the glacier, on slow and rough terrain. The views get more impressive as you climb towards todays high pass. From the top you see the full vista behind you, and you finally see grass and beaches instead of scree ahead. 

<p align="center" width="100%">
    <img width="400" src="/images/huemul2a.jpeg">
</p>

The descent is rotten. Steep mud, slippery roots, and loose rock the entire way down. Even in mostly dry conditions it had me cursing out loud at points. Calling this the hardest hike in Patagonia is probably an exaggeration, but this descent helps the case. As you decsend you forget about the icy glaciers and scree of the past days, and look ahead to green peninsulas on huge lakes with icebergs floating in them.

I found a great spot beside the lake, setup my tent, dried my gear in the sun, and sat with my feet in the water. It was one of the nicest camp spots imaginable and made todays hardship worthwhile. Only two tents were setup there as everyone else camped higher near the main trail. That evening strong rain and wind returned so that meant another movie night, which was no harm.

## Day 3
#### Bahia de los Témpanos to El Chaltén
*Distance: 25.2km | Ascent: 920m*

I got such a fright when I woke up. A mouse had chewed through my tent overnight and eaten my wraps. This happened to me years earlier on the [West Highland Way](west-highland-way) and apparently I still hadn’t learned my lesson about storing food beside lakes. My panic while checking that he wasn't in my bag would have been funny to see.

The morning was gloomy and windy. I moved quickly through grassy valleys, muddy bogs, and long beaches while fog slowly lifted from the mountains. The second tyrolean traverse had a queue where people struggled to get on and off the cable. Luckily they let me pass which I did quickly. It was much more chill than the last one.

<p align="center" width="100%">
    <img width="400" src="/images/huemul3a.jpeg">

The final section went on forever. Strong headwinds felt like a treadmill, especially on the last few kilometres of road into town. Eventually El Chaltén appeared, bringing a warm shower and real food.

I celebrated with a burger and a pint before returning to the hostel feeling very proud of the last few days. That night the storm arrived properly and hammered the town until morning. Timing wise, it couldn’t have worked out better.

## Afterthoughts

The Huemul Circuit is a brilliant hike. Harder than most trails in Patagonia, but probably not as terrifying as people online make it sound. Still, it shouldn’t be underestimated. There’s no signal for most of the route and rescue would be a long way away if something went wrong.

The tyrolean traverses, glacier travel, steep muddy descents and brutal weather all add genuine challenge. Strong winds and freezing temperatures make camp life feel serious too. But that’s exactly what makes the route memorable.

More than anything, I loved how remote it felt. Compared to the crowds around Fitz Roy and Torres Del Paine, the Huemul still feels adventurous. The final campsite beside the lake is one of the prettiest places I’ve ever pitched a tent.
